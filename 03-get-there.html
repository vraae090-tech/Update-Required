How to get there?

How to find your way with Maps, and how to pay for the subway and the bus.

—Senior-friendly Guide

Where is the map?

Your phone came with a map already on it. On an iPhone it is called Maps, and its icon is a small square of streets with a road curving across it. You do not have to download anything, and you do not have to pay for it.

If you cannot see it on your home screen, you do not have to go hunting through pages of icons. Swipe down from the middle of the screen and a search field appears at the top. Type map into it — three letters is enough — and the app will be the first thing in the list.

Finding a place

  1. Tap the search field. It is the white bar across the bottom of the map, and it usually says Search for a place or address.
  2. Type as little as you can get away with. You do not need the full address. A name and a neighbourhood is plenty, and often the name on its own is enough.
  3. Pick it off the list. Results appear as you type. Tap the right one and the map moves to it.

If nothing you want comes up, the usual reason is a spelling the phone does not recognise. Delete a word rather than adding one — shorter searches find more, not less.

  • grand central
  • pharmacy near me
  • 241 w 14th

The places you go to most

There are two or three addresses you will look up more than all the others put together. Maps lets you save them so you never type them again. They sit as round buttons under the search field, and one tap starts the directions.

  1. Search for the place the way you just did.
  2. Swipe up on the white panel that slides in from the bottom with the place's name on it.
  3. Tap Add to Favourites. It is now a circle of its own, waiting under the search field.

Save the places you go, not the places you might.

Moving the map

The map is bigger than the screen, always. Three movements are all you need, and none of them can break anything — the map cannot be put somewhere it will not come back from.

  1. 01Drag. Put one finger on the map and move it. The map follows your finger, like sliding a paper map across a table.
  2. 02Pinch to zoom. Put two fingers down and move them apart to come closer; bring them together to go further away. If two fingers is awkward, tap twice quickly with one finger instead — that zooms in too.
  3. 03Find yourself again. There is a small arrow in the corner. Tap it and the map comes back to where you are standing. This is the way out of being lost on the map, and it is worth learning before the other two.

Three ways to look at the same map

The same map can be drawn three different ways, and you choose which. Tap the small stack of squares in the top right corner and the three appear. Nothing you pick here can get you lost — it is the same map underneath, every time.

  1. 01Map. Streets, parks and shop names, drawn. This is the one it starts on and the one to leave it on.
  2. 02Transit. The subway and bus lines drawn over the top, each in its own colour, with the stations marked. This is the one to switch to before working out how to get somewhere.
  3. 03Satellite. Photographs from above instead of a drawing. Good for recognising a building before you reach it; harder to read for anything else.
Try the three views

Press each one. Nothing moves and nothing is sent anywhere — the line underneath just says what that view is for.

The everyday one. Streets, parks and shop names, and the one to leave it on. If you are not sure which view you are in, this is probably it.

Now you pick

Six places to be. Which of the three views would you put the map in? Press one and find out — nothing is sent anywhere, and you can run through them as many times as you like.

  1. You are sitting at home and you want to know which subway line will get you to a hospital on the other side of town.

    Transit. The lines are drawn over the streets in their own colours with the stations marked, so you can follow one with a finger. Map knows the streets but not the trains.

  2. You have come up out of the subway and you have no idea which street you are standing on.

    Map. Street names, written on the streets. This is the one it starts on and the one to come back to when the only thing you want is where am I.

  3. You are going to a dentist you have never been to, and you would like to know what the door looks like before you are standing outside it.

    Satelite. It is photographs from above rather than a drawing, so a building looks like itself. That is the one thing it is better at than the other two.

  4. You want to know whether the bus stop at the end of your street is on a line that goes anywhere near the library.

    Transit. Bus routes are drawn in as well as the subway, each in its own colour, so you can see where a stop leads before you stand at it.

  5. You want to know whether there is a pharmacy on the next block.

    Map. Shop names are drawn on it. Satelite would show you a roof, and a roof does not tell you what is underneath it.

  6. Someone has told you to meet them “in the park, by the tennis courts”, and you want to see where the courts actually are.

    Satelite. The park is a green shape on Map; in a photograph from above you can count the courts and see which gate is nearest to them.

The information mark

A letter i in a circle means there is more here if you want it. It is never a warning and never a thing you have to deal with. Tapping it opens an explanation; tapping anywhere else closes it again.

It is worth knowing because it is the same mark everywhere — on the map, in Settings, in your bank's app. Once you have met it once you have met it everywhere.

  • Information

    A letter i in a circle. There is an explanation behind it if you want one, and nothing at all happens if you never tap it.

  • The three views

    What appears once you have tapped it: Map, Transit and Satellite, side by side, waiting to be chosen between. Tapping one changes the drawing, not the place.

A circled i is an offer, not an instruction.

Before you set off

The worst place to work out a journey is standing on a platform. Do it at the kitchen table instead, where you can take as long as you like and there is nobody waiting behind you.

  1. Search for where you are going and tap Directions.
  2. Tap the small bus or train symbol to ask for the journey by public transport rather than by car.
  3. Read the first line only. It says how long it takes and which line to get. Everything underneath is detail you can come back to.
  4. Write down the two things you will forget: the line, and the stop you get off at. A phone in your pocket is not a phone you are reading.

Decide at the table. Not on the platform.

Paying to get there: the MetroCard

  1. Hold it with the arrow pointing away from you. The card itself tells you: Insert this way, printed beside an arrow, and This way facing you on the side that should be looking back at you.
  2. Slide it into the slot on your right in one steady movement. Not fast, not slow.
  3. Take it out and push. The small screen says GO and how much is left.
  4. If it says swipe again, wait a second and do it the same way. It is almost always the speed, not the card.
  • Buy and refill them at the machines in any station. The machines take cash and cards.
  • A card can hold either money or an unlimited pass, not both at once.
  • They expire. The date is printed on the back, in small type.
  • If it stops working with money still on it, take it to a station booth rather than throwing it away.

OMNY, and the reduced fare

OMNY is the newer way to pay. Instead of sliding a card into a slot, you hold it flat against the reader on top of the turnstile and wait for the beep. There is nothing to insert and nothing to get the wrong way round.

If you are 65 or over, you are entitled to pay half. You have to apply for it once, and then you hold your own card against the same reader as everybody else. Nobody can tell which card you are holding, and nothing about using it is different.

  • OMNY

    The ordinary card. Anybody can buy one at a machine or a booth, and it costs the full fare.

  • Reduced fare

    The same card, applied for once, charging half. It is held against the same reader and nobody can see which one you have.

  • You can also tap a contactless bank card or a phone on the same reader, at the full fare.
  • Tap the same card every time. The system counts your rides and stops charging you once you have paid for a weekly pass.
  • One tap per person. A card cannot be passed back for somebody else.
  • The reduced fare has to be applied for before it works. A station booth will tell you how, and so will the number on the back of a MetroCard.

If it goes wrong on the way

None of these mean you have done something wrong, and none of them need anybody's help to fix. They are the things that go wrong most, and what to do about each one standing right there.

If something goes wrong
If the map says you are in the wrong place:
Walk a few steps into the open and wait. A phone works out where it is from the sky, and it cannot see much of it indoors or between tall buildings.
If the turnstile will not let you through:
Look at the small screen. It will say either insufficient fare, which means money, or just used, which means wait two minutes and try again.
If the reader does not beep:
Hold the card flatter and closer, and hold it still for a second. It is a contact that has to be made, not a gesture.
If you have got on going the wrong way:
Get off at the next stop and cross to the other platform. On most lines you will not have to pay again.
If none of that works:
There is a person in the booth at the station entrance. Asking them is not a last resort.
